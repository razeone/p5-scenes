## How to use this document

Each entry maps a compositional idea from the painting to a concrete camera/blocking setup. Pair this with [transfiguration-raphael.md](./transfiguration-raphael.md) for the underlying symbolism, and with [cinema-references.md](./cinema-references.md) for filmed precedents of similar staging.

## Upper-register shots (the "revelation" language)

* **Wide, low-angle hero shot**: subject centered and elevated above the frame's midline, arms open or raised, lit from a single overhead/off-frame source so the body reads as the brightest element in the shot.
* **Symmetrical flanking two-shot**: two secondary characters positioned equidistant left/right of the hero, angled inward, in conversation with the elevated figure — mirrors Moses/Elijah flanking Christ.
* **Reaction triptych**: three witnesses in a shallow horizontal line beneath the hero, each with a distinct physical reaction (shielding eyes, recoiling, prostrate) — useful as a three-shot or a quick cutting pattern of three singles.
* **Blown-out backlight**: practical or off-camera source pushed to near-clip behind the elevated figure, crushing the background into soft cloud-like shapes (achievable with diffusion + overexposure or in-camera ND pull).
* **Weightless staging**: rigging, wire work, slow-motion, or subtle upward dolly to remove the sense of ground contact from the elevated figure.

## Lower-register shots (the "chaos" language)

* **Low, chaotic wide**: handheld or Dutch-angle wide shot, figures at varied heights and diagonals, negative space minimized — deliberately the visual opposite of the upper-register symmetry.
* **Contorted close-up**: extreme close-up on a single figure mid-crisis (twisted posture, rolled-back eyes, open mouth) lit with hard, directional side light to maximize shadow depth.
* **Supporting-figure two-shot**: a caretaker or parent physically bracing the figure in crisis, camera low enough to put both faces in frame with the crisis figure slightly higher or more central.
* **The interlocutor shot**: one figure breaks eye-line toward camera/lens, gesturing off-frame — use as a direct-address insert or a look-to-camera beat that bridges scenes for the audience.
* **Foreground bridge figure**: a compositionally separate character (not clearly part of either group) placed in the extreme foreground, softly lit, back or three-quarter to camera, physically linking two clusters of characters in a single frame.

## Lighting patterns

* **Split lighting scheme**: hard key + minimal fill on the "crisis" space; soft, high-key, diffused top light on the "revelation" space. Keep the contrast ratio between the two spaces as extreme as the format allows.
* **Practical moon/water motif**: a reflective surface (water, glass, metal) positioned low in frame as a recurring omen before a crisis beat, lit with a cool, desaturated source.
* **Color-coded witness triad**: if blocking three witnesses/characters together, assign each a distinct, consistent costume color (e.g., blue-yellow, green, red) carried across the whole film as a visual leitmotif.

## Composition and lens notes

* Favor a pyramidal/triangular arrangement of principal figures in "revelation" scenes, with the vertex at or near the elevated figure's head.
* Use a wider lens and lower camera height for "chaos" scenes to introduce mild distortion and instability; use a longer lens and eye-level or slightly elevated camera for "revelation" scenes to flatten and stabilize the frame.
* Reserve a hard vertical or horizontal line in the frame (architecture, horizon, doorway) to visually separate the two registers when both need to appear in a single wide shot.

## Sequencing idea

* Consider crosscutting or a single continuous crane move that travels from the chaotic lower space up into the calm elevated space, visually enacting the painting's implied vertical relationship between suffering and intervention.
